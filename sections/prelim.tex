\section{Preliminaries}

\paragraph{Notation.}
We use $\lambda \in \mathbb{Z}$ to denote the security parameter.
We use $x \gets y$ to denote the assignment of the value of $y$ to $x$,
and write $x \rgets S$ to denote sampling an element from the set $S$ independently and uniformly at random.
For a randomized algorithm $\adv$ we write $y \rgets \adv(x)$ to denote the random variable
that is the output of $\adv(x)$ on input $x$.
%
We use $\set{n}$ for the set $\{1,\ldots,n\}$.
%
For a positive integer $q$ we use $\Zq$ to denote the ring $\Z/q\Z$.

When writing protocols,
$\require$ denotes the clause that if some predicate~$\cdot$ does not hold,
then the respective party must abort the protocol.

\paragraph{Hash Functions.}
Our construction uses three hash functions, modeled as random oracles in the security proof:
$\HashSid \colon (\zo^\lambda)^n \to \zo^\lambda$, used to derive the joint session identifier from the
parties' contributions;
$\HashCom \colon \zo^* \to \Zq$, used to commit to a party's coefficient-commitment vector; and
$\HashSchnorrR \colon \zo^* \to \Zq$, the Fiat--Shamir challenge hash of the proof system.
The codomains of $\HashCom$ and $\HashSchnorrR$ are the scalar field $\Zq$
(the output of $\HashSchnorrR$ is used as an exponent).

\subsection{Polynomial Interpolation and Shamir Secret Sharing}
\label{prelim:secret-sharing}

A polynomial $f(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + \ldots + a_{\tcor} x^{\tcor}$
of degree $\tcor$ over a field $\F$ can be interpolated by $\thresh$ points.
Let $\eta \subseteq \set{n}$ be the list of $\thresh$ distinct indices
corresponding to the $x$-coordinates $x_i \in \F$ of these points.
Then the Lagrange polynomial $L_i(x)$ has the form:

\[
  L_i(x) = \prod_{j \in \eta; j \neq i } \frac{x-x_j}{x_i - x_j}
\]


Given a set of $\thresh$ points $(x_i, f(x_i))_{i \in \eta}$,
any point $f(\xtilde)$ on the polynomial $f$ can be determined by Lagrange interpolation as follows:
\[
  f(\xtilde) = \sum_{k \in \eta} f(x_k) \cdot L_k(\xtilde)
\]

\paragraph{Shamir Secret Sharing.}
Shamir secret sharing~\cite{Shamir79} is an $(n, \thresh)$-threshold secret sharing scheme consisting of algorithms $(\IssueShares, \allowbreak \Recover)$, defined as follows:

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1mm]
    \item $\IssueShares(\secret, n, \thresh) \rightarrow \{ (1, \share_1), \ldots, (n, \share_n)\}$:
      On input a secret $\secret$, number of participants $n$, and threshold $\thresh$, perform the following.
First, define a polynomial $f(Z) = x + a_1 Z + a_2 Z^2 + \cdots + a_{\tcor} Z^{\tcor}$ with $a_1, \ldots, a_{\tcor} \randpick \Zq$.
Then, set each participant's share $\share_i, i \in \set{n}$, to be the evaluation of $f(i)$:
    \[
        \share_i \gets \secret + \sum_{j \in \set{\tcor}} a_j i^j
      \]
%
    Output $\{(i, \share_i) \}_{i \in \set{n}}$.

  \item $ \Recover(\thresh, \{ (i, \share_i)\}_{ i \in \coalition}) \rightarrow \bot / \secret$:
    On input threshold $\thresh$ and a set of shares
$\{(i, \share) \}_{i \in \coalition}$,
    output $\bot$ if the coalition $\coalition \not\subseteq \set{n}$ or if
$\lvert \coalition \rvert < \thresh$.
Otherwise, recover $\secret$ as follows:
  \[
      \secret \gets \sum_{i \in \coalition} \share_i \cdot L_i(0)
  \]
    where the Lagrange coefficient for the set
$\coalition$ is defined by
    \[
    L_i(0) = \prod_{j \in \coalition, j\neq i} \frac{j}{j - i}
    \]
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Communication Model}
\label{sec:comm-model}

When describing distributed key generation schemes,
we assume each pair of parties can communicate over a secure channel.
We introduce below two types of secure channels that we rely upon for our DKG construction.


All our results are presented in the synchronous network model.
Concretely, we require that protocol execution progresses in well-defined rounds, with every message sent in a given round being delivered before the start of the subsequent round.
We assume that the adversary can be rushing, meaning that in any given Round~$r$ the corrupted parties can receive all messages sent by the honest parties in Round~$r$ before the corrupted parties are required to send their Round-$r$ messages.

\medskip
\noindent\emph{Note.} In practice, we adopt Goldwasser and Lindell's statement~\cite{GoldwasserLindell02}: ``when output delivery is not guaranteed, asynchronous communication does not present any additional hardship. Specifically, the honest parties should merely wait until receiving all round $i$ messages before sending their message for round $i+1$. This ensures that the only difference between a synchronous and an asynchronous execution is the ability of the adversary to prevent the parties from receiving output. When output delivery is not guaranteed, this is anyway allowed.''

\paragraph{Peer-to-Peer Channels}
A peer-to-peer channel allows a party to send a message to one specific other party
over a secure point-to-point link, with the guarantee of authenticity, confidentiality,
and termination\footnote{If the sender is faulty, the receiver obtains a special $\bot$ value that is treated as an abort.}:
the recipient is assured of the sender's identity and the integrity of the message,
and no other party can read or tamper with its contents.
We denote sending a message to a single party over such a channel with $\send$.

\paragraph{Broadcast Channels}
A broadcast channel sends one message to all other players,
with the guarantee that all players received the same message.
For our DKG construction we rely on a \emph{terminating} and \emph{reliable} broadcast channel, which
guarantees the properties of Validity, No duplication, Integrity and Totality
(see Module 3.11 and Module 3.12 of~\cite{CGR11}), together with \emph{Termination}: every correct
party eventually delivers some value, possibly the special symbol $\bot$ indicating a faulty or
timed-out sender, which is treated as an abort.
We denote sending over a broadcast channel with $\broadcast$. Note that on the MPC level,
these channels are equipped with timeouts.

We instantiate this reliable broadcast following the book of Cachin, Guerraoui and
Rodrigues~\cite{CGR11}; concretely, we use their ``Authenticated Double-Echo Broadcast''
protocol (Algorithm 3.18 of~\cite{CGR11}) to achieve Byzantine reliable broadcast.
The file \textit{echo\_broadcast.rs} contains the implementation of the reliable broadcast protocol.
We essentially follow the same algorithm 3.18 of~\cite{CGR11} with three mild differences:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If a party is broadcasting a value, then the protocol expects that other parties would also want to broadcast some values. This is essentially equivalent to running $n$ parallel instances of algorithm 3.18 where one instance where party $k$ is a sender and $n-1$ instances where $k$ is a receiver. Such an implementation serves directly our use cases in protocols where symmetry holds between participants. Additionally, this implementation prevents having deadlocks (two parties waiting for each others to send values on two different broadcast channels) and prevents having inefficient sequential broadcast instances.
\item If an (honest) broadcast sender recovers an output at the end of the protocol that is different than
the value it input, then it aborts considering that the effective number of malicious parties is larger than what the
protocol allows.
\item If a party collects a number of votes and deduces that there is no way to meet the necessary threshold for any of
the collected votes, then it immediately aborts instead of waiting for timeouts to abort the process.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Terminating Reliable Broadcast}
In the final round of our protocol we name the terminating reliable broadcast channel above
explicitly as a \emph{terminating reliable broadcast} (TRB), which we denote by $\TRB$, and
additionally require it to provide \emph{Agreement}, so that all correct parties deliver the
same value. Concretely, $\TRB$ satisfies
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Validity}: if a correct sender broadcasts a value $m$, then every correct party eventually delivers $m$;
\item \emph{Integrity}: every correct party delivers at most one value, and if it delivers $m \neq \bot$ then $m$ was broadcast by the sender;
\item \emph{Agreement}: if some correct party delivers $m$, then every correct party eventually delivers $m$;
\item \emph{Termination}: every correct party eventually delivers some value, possibly the distinguished symbol $\bot$ indicating a faulty (or timed-out) sender.
\end{itemize}
The Termination property is precisely what lets the protocol make progress under asynchrony:
a party that never receives an expected message simply times out, and the timeout is treated
as a failure event so that the corresponding $\TRB$ instance delivers $\bot$ rather than blocking.
We use $\TRB$ to agree on whether key generation succeeded: each party broadcasts
$(\success, \sid)$ on its own $\TRB$ instance, so that every party receives $n$ delivered values, one
per sender. By Termination, each delivered value is either $(\success, \sid)$ or the distinguished
symbol $\bot$ (indicating a faulty or timed-out sender). A party outputs its keys only if all
$n$ delivered values equal $(\success, \sid)$, and aborts otherwise.

\paragraph{Optimizations}
The description above assumes idealized synchronous channels that terminate within each round.
In a real deployment the network is asynchronous and a broadcast may fail to terminate on its own;
we then proceed as follows. For the intermediate rounds, honest parties simply wait until they have
received all round-$i$ messages before sending their round-$(i+1)$ messages, so that the only
additional power the adversary gains is to withhold output, which is anyway allowed when output
delivery is not guaranteed~\cite{GoldwasserLindell02}. For the final success broadcast we obtain
termination from a blockchain: each party posts its value $(\success, \sid)$ on chain, and the parties
wait for a fixed number of epochs. The blockchain provides agreement and persistence on the posted
values, while the fixed epoch window plays the role of the timeout---a party whose value is not on
chain within that window is treated as faulty, so the instance terminates and delivers $\bot$ rather
than blocking indefinitely.

\subsection{Distributed Key Generation}
\label{def:dgk}

We follow the definition and idealized notion of security $\FDKGAbort$
for a distributed key generation (DKG) protocol where the adversary is allowed to bias the output key with some (constrained) additive  shift $\Delta$,
as presented by Kate et al.~\cite{KMSSSY26}.
We give a stand-alone definition of security for $\FDKGAbort$.
Because our proof for $\pedpopplus$ uses only online straight-line simulation,
our proof with respect to $\FDKGAbort$ in the standalone setting is also by extension secure in the Universal Composability (UC) framework~\cite{KushilevitzLR10}.
We assume an adversary that performs only static corruptions.

\subsection{Online-Extractable Zero Knowledge Proof System}
\label{def:online-extractable}
Our protocol requires an \emph{online-extractable} (straight-line extractable)
non-interactive zero-knowledge proof for the relation $\mathcal{R}_P$ defined below.
Such a proof system is what UC security demands here: because rewinding is not
permitted in the UC framework, the simulator must recover each corrupt party's
committed contribution online, without rewinding. We therefore assume a proof
system $\NIZKUCExtractable$ that is zero-knowledge and online-extractable for
$\mathcal{R}_P$ in the programmable random-oracle model, and we instantiate it
concretely in Figure~\ref{fg:concrete-simulator}. A construction in the style
of~\cite{BCK25} follows.

The relation $\mathcal{R}_P$ certifies that an ElGamal ciphertext $c=(C_0,C_1)$
correctly encrypts $g^{\sk}$ together with knowledge of the exponent $\sk$
satisfying $\PK = g^{\sk}$. The verifiable-encryption component is what makes the proof
online-extractable: the simulator samples the setup element $h = g^{\eta}$, keeps
the trapdoor $\eta$, and decrypts $c$ to recover $g^{\sk}$ without rewinding.

\[
  \mathcal{R}_P = \Big\{\, \big(g, h, \PK, C_0, C_1\big) ; (\mathsf{sk}, r) \ :\
    \PK = g^{\sk} \ \wedge\ C_0 = g^{r} \ \wedge\ C_1 = h^{r}\cdot g^{\sk} \,\Big\}.
\]

The witness is the pair $(\sk, r)$; the statement consists of the generator $g$ of $\Gr$, the setup element
$h = g^{\eta} \rgets \Gr$, the public key $\PK$, and the ElGamal ciphertext $c = (C_0, C_1)$ of $g^{\sk}$ under
randomness $r$. Extraction uses the third conjunct: knowing the trapdoor $\eta$ (where $h = g^{\eta}$), the
simulator recovers $g^{\sk} = C_1 \cdot C_0^{-\eta}$ without rewinding.

Because the statement binds two independent witnesses, $\sk$ and $r$, the proof is a generalized Schnorr
(AND) proof with \emph{two} responses; a single response cannot open both. The prover samples nonces
$a, b \rgets \Zq$ and forms the commitments
\[
  T_1 = g^{a}, \qquad T_2 = g^{b}, \qquad T_3 = h^{b}\cdot g^{a}.
\]
Following the Fiat--Shamir transform, the challenge is derived as
\[
  c' = \HashSchnorrR(g, h, \PK, C_0, C_1, T_1, T_2, T_3, \gamma),
\]
where $\gamma$ is the proof context (e.g.\@ $\gamma = (\sid, k, C_k[0])$ in the protocol), and the prover
sends the two responses $z_1 = a + c'\sk$ and $z_2 = b + c' r$. The verifier accepts iff
\begin{align*}
  (1)\quad & g^{z_1} = T_1 \cdot \PK^{c'} \\
  (2)\quad & g^{z_2} = T_2 \cdot C_0^{c'} \\
  (3)\quad & h^{z_2}\cdot g^{z_1} = T_3 \cdot C_1^{c'}
\end{align*}
Equation~(1) is a Schnorr proof of knowledge of the exponent $\sk$ with $\PK = g^{\sk}$, while equations~(2)--(3) certify that
$(C_0, C_1)$ encrypts $g^{\sk}$ under the same $\sk$ and randomness $r$.

The three commitments need not be transmitted: it suffices to send $\pi = (c', z_1, z_2)$, since the verifier
recomputes $T_1 = g^{z_1}\PK^{-c'}$, $T_2 = g^{z_2}C_0^{-c'}$, and $T_3 = h^{z_2}g^{z_1}C_1^{-c'}$ and
re-derives $c'$ from the hash. The proof is then three scalars beyond the ciphertext $(C_0, C_1)$, which is
transmitted anyway for extraction. The $\Prove$ and $\Verify$ algorithms are detailed in
Figure~\ref{fg:concrete-simulator}.
